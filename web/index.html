How the cascade works

        

          
A wrong date here is the worst thing this tool could do to you. Every window below is a condition of the offence or a period of limitation. Miss one and the complaint is gone, however good the rest of the case is. So the arithmetic is calendar-correct — short months, leap days, month ends — and wherever the law or a date of service is genuinely uncertain, the tool reports the earlier of the defensible deadlines as the one to work to, with the outer limit beside it.

          
    
            
  1. Presentation — s.138 proviso (a). The cheque must reach the bank within six months of its date or within its validity, whichever expires first. Since the Reserve Bank reduced cheque validity to three months in 2012, the three months is normally the operative limit.
    2. 
            
  2. Demand notice — s.138 proviso (b). A written demand for the cheque amount must be given to the drawer within thirty days of the payee receiving the bank's information about the return. The tool counts from the memo date, which is the earliest that window can close.
    3. 
            
  3. The drawer's fifteen days — s.138 proviso (c). From the drawer's receipt of the notice. The offence is complete only on the day after they expire unpaid, and that day is the cause of action.
    4. 
            
  4. The complaint — s.142(1)(b). One calendar month from the cause of action. Not thirty days: a cause of action arising on 1 February gives a deadline of 1 March, while thirty days would compute 3 March and file out of time.
    5. 
            
  5. The civil suit — Limitation Act, 1963. Three years from the dishonour. It is on the page because a missed criminal window is not necessarily a lost debt.
    6. 
          

          
Where the notice has gone out but the acknowledgement has not come back, service is a guess — and if the drawer in fact received it sooner than assumed, the complaint window closes sooner too. So the tool also computes the deadline on the assumption that the notice arrived the day it was posted, and tells you to work to that. Filing by it is in time whatever the court later finds about service.

          

            Assumptions and figures
            
    
            
      
              
    • The day a period runs from is excluded, under s.9 of the General Clauses Act, 1897.
      • 
              
    • One month is a month of the British calendar, under s.3(35) of the General Clauses Act, 1897: 28, 29, 30 or 31 days. Month arithmetic clamps to the end of the target month, so one month from 31 January is 28 or 29 February and never 2 March.
      • 
              
    • The complaint deadline shown is one month from the expiry of the drawer's fifteen days. The outer limit shown beside it is one month from the cause of action itself, which is the reckoning in Econ Antri Ltd v. Rom Industries Ltd, (2014) 11 SCC 769, affirming Saketh India Ltd v. India Securities Ltd, (1999) 3 SCC 1. Where they differ it is by a day. Work to the earlier one.
      • 
              
    • Days in the post is this tool's assumption and not a statutory period. Replace it with the real date of service the moment the acknowledgement or the tracking record arrives.
      • 
              
    • A deadline falling on a weekend is flagged but not moved. Section 4 of the Limitation Act, 1963 allows a filing on the day the court reopens where the last day falls on a day the court is closed — plan to be early rather than to rely on it.
      • 
              
    • The proviso to s.142(1)(b) lets a court take cognizance after the period on sufficient cause being shown. That is an indulgence to ask for, never a date to plan around.
      • 
            
    
          
    
          
    This is a tracker, not legal advice. It computes and prints; it never sends, serves or files anything, and it has no way to. Confirm the current statutory periods and the facts and dates of your own case with an advocate before you act on any date it shows.
